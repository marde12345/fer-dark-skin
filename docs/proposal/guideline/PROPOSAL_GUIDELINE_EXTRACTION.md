## 1. Philosophy of Design Research

**Location: BAB 1 §1.1–1.2 (p.10–14), BAB 2 whole chapter (p.28–43).**

- Research has two complementary orientations, not two tiers of legitimacy: **understanding phenomena** (what/why/how do things relate — description, explanation, prediction, theory) and **designing solutions** (how should X be designed to improve a condition) (p.10–12, citing March & Smith 1995, Simon 1996, Hevner et al. 2004, Wieringa 2014).
- Design research is defined as research that **deliberately integrates** problem understanding, artifact design, artifact evaluation, and knowledge formation (p.12).
- Design research has two goals beyond "the artifact was built": (1) **improvement** — the artifact must benefit stakeholders or a class of problem (p.13, Hevner et al. 2004); (2) **knowledge contribution** — findings must be abstracted beyond one instance into design principles, models, methods, mechanism–context–outcome relationships (p.13–14, Gregor & Hevner 2013; Gregor & Jones 2007).
- **Philosophical foundations (BAB 2)**:
  - *Philosophy of science* (§2.1, p.28–30): Popper's falsifiability (critical testing, not just confirmation-seeking) and Kuhn's paradigm concept (research communities operate inside a conceptual frame that shapes questions/methods/interpretation) are both cited as relevant to how design research claims knowledge status.
  - *Ontology* (§2.2, p.30–32): design research's object of study includes not only existing phenomena but also **deliberately created artifacts** (constructs, models, methods, systems…). Table 2.1 (p.32) gives 8 ontological questions a proposal should be able to answer (what is the object of study, what entities are involved, what is current/desired state, etc.).
  - *Epistemology* (§2.3, p.32–34): design research obtains knowledge not only by observing what exists but through **building and evaluating** something new. Distinguishes *descriptive* knowledge (what/why something happens) from *prescriptive* knowledge (how something can be designed) — prescriptive knowledge about artifacts is not simply reducible to descriptive theory (Iivari 2007).
  - *Axiology* (§2.4, p.34–36): design research is not value-free — "better" is never automatic or neutral (a faster system is not automatically better if it reduces fairness). Table 2.2 (p.34) lists 8 example values (efficiency, effectiveness, fairness, transparency, privacy, safety, accessibility, sustainability) each with an axiological question and a design implication. Ethics is described as closely tied to axiology and can generate **ethical requirements** that shape artifact structure from the start (p.35).
  - *The Sciences of the Artificial* (§2.5, p.36–38): Simon's foundational idea that design is a transition from an **existing situation to a preferred situation**, and design is a **search process in a solution space** under bounded rationality (satisficing, not perfect optimization) (p.36–37).
  - *Research paradigms* (§2.6, p.38–39): positivism, postpositivism, interpretivism, critical, pragmatism, and design science are presented (Table 2.3, p.38) as potentially combinable rather than mutually exclusive, as long as there is argumentative/methodological consistency.
  - *Position of design research in modern science* (§2.7, p.39–41): design research bridges *understanding* and *intervention*, *theory* and *artifact*, *knowledge* and *practice*, in a cycle: **understanding → designing → evaluating → learning** (p.41).

## 2. Proposal Architecture

**Location: BAB 1 §1.6–1.7 (p.21–26); BAB 14 §14.1 (p.209–211).**

- A design-research proposal is an **academic argument**, not a list of planned activities (p.21). Six characteristics of a strong proposal (§1.6, p.21–23, Table 1.4 p.22):
  1. **Problem relevance** — supported by evidence.
  2. **Existing knowledge, existing solutions, and a clear gap.**
  3. **A targeted artifact** — need not be fully realized at proposal stage, but type, purpose, mechanism, and rationale must be explainable.
  4. **Evaluability** — how success/limitation will be tested must be stated from the start.
  5. **Knowledge contribution** — positioned against problem-domain/solution-domain maturity (Gregor & Hevner 2013).
  6. **Traceability and consistency** — problem → RQ → objective → requirement → artifact → evaluation → contribution must read as one unbroken argument chain, with no logical jump (p.23).
- **General proposal structure** (§1.7, Table 1.5, p.24–25): context → background (current state, evidence, impact) → problem statement → literature review → state of the art → gap → research questions → research objectives → design requirements → proposed artifact → design & development → demonstration → evaluation → contribution → research plan → ethics & risk.
- **Chapter-14 restated architecture** (Table 14.1, p.210): the whole proposal answers, in sequence: *Why this problem?* → *Why research?* → *What will be answered?* (RQ) → *What will be achieved?* (Objectives) → *What must the solution do?* (Requirements) → *What will be designed?* (Artifact) → *Why should it work?* (Theory/justificatory knowledge) → *How will it be built?* (Development methodology) → *How will it be tested?* (Evaluation plan) → *What is expected to improve?* (Practical contribution) → *What is expected to be learned?* (Knowledge contribution). Summarized as **Why → What → How → Evidence → Contribution** (p.211).
- The master end-to-end logic chain stated repeatedly across the book (p.16, p.129, p.211, p.221): **Problem → Evidence → Existing Knowledge/Solutions → State of the Art → Gap → Research Question → Objective → Knowledge/Theory → Design Requirements → Artifact → Development → Demonstration → Evaluation → Learning → Contribution.**

## 3. Problem Formulation

**Location: BAB 4, whole chapter (p.68–87).**

- Chain: **context → problematic situation → evidence → root causes → stakeholder goals → practical problem → knowledge problem → research problem → research scope** (p.70, restated as the chapter's organizing structure).
- §4.1 **Phenomenon and context** (p.68–71): a phenomenon is not automatically a problem; a symptom is not automatically the root cause. Every phenomenon description should answer: *what is happening, where, who is affected, under what conditions, why does it matter* (Table 4.1, p.70).
- §4.2 **Practical problem** (p.71–73, citing Wieringa 2010/2014): a practical problem = the **gap between a stakeholder's goal and their actual experienced state** (current state + desired state + gap, all three required). Problems are **goal-relative** — what is a problem for one stakeholder may not be for another. A missing technology or missing feature ("we don't have a dashboard", "we don't use AI") is *not itself* a practical problem — it is a candidate solution; the underlying unmet need must be identified first (p.72).
- §4.3 **Knowledge problem** (p.73–76, Wieringa 2010/2014): distinguishes *practical problem* (demands change in the world) from *knowledge question* (demands new knowledge about the world). A knowledge gap is **not** the same as "no papers exist on this" — administrative literature-gap claims ("belum ada penelitian di Indonesia") are explicitly rejected as insufficient (p.74). Table 4.3 (p.76) types knowledge questions as descriptive, explanatory, comparative, predictive, evaluative, mechanism-oriented, boundary-oriented.
- §4.4 **Root-cause analysis** (p.76–78): symptoms ≠ root causes. Techniques: Ishikawa/cause-and-effect diagrams (treated as hypotheses to verify, not final proof) and Five Whys (used cautiously on complex problems, combined with process analysis/data/interviews). Chain: **problem → symptoms → contributing factors → root causes → intervention points** (Table 4.4, p.77) — an "intervention point" is a factor that can realistically be influenced by the artifact; factors outside the artifact's reach are treated as **constraints**, not requirements sources.
- §4.5 **Stakeholder analysis** (p.78–80, Freeman 1984; Bryson 2004; Wieringa 2014): stakeholder analysis must identify problem owner, goals, conflicts, influence/power, and desired effects — not just "who to interview." Power-vs-interest mapping recommended. Template: stakeholder → role → problem/concern → goal → influence → impact → potential requirement (Table 4.5, p.79–80).
- §4.6 **Problem statement formulation** (p.80–82): built in 6 steps — current state+evidence → desired state/stakeholder goals → consequence of the gap → causal mechanism → existing knowledge/solutions and their limitation → conclusion of why research is needed. A **weak** example and a **strong rewritten** example are given (p.80–81). Problem statement must not prematurely lock in a technology/solution (**neutrality** criterion). Quality checklist (Table 4.6, p.82): specificity, evidence, stakeholder relevance, desired state, consequence, root cause, existing solutions, knowledge gap, design opportunity, neutrality, researchability, traceability.
- §4.7 **Scope** (p.83–85): scope is deliberately chosen, not a weakness. Distinguishes **scope** (deliberate inclusion decision) from **assumption** (condition presumed true), **constraint** (external limiting condition, e.g. regulation/budget), and **limitation** (a weakness affecting conclusion strength, e.g. single-organization evaluation). Boundary dimensions: domain, organization, stakeholder, technology, data, evaluation, geography, time (Table 4.7, p.84).

## 4. Evidence

**Location: BAB 5 §5.3 (p.93–95); referenced throughout BAB 4–8.**

- Evidence must match the specific claim it supports — a "symptom" evidence (e.g., high service time) does not by itself prove a "cause" claim (e.g., "caused by lack of automation") (p.93–94). Confusing correlation/perception with a causal claim can misdirect the intervention point (p.94).
- Evidence quality is checked on: **source, relevance, accuracy, recency, representativeness** (p.93).
- Preliminary evidence at proposal stage need not be as complete as the eventual main study's data, but must be sufficient to justify that the problem is worth researching — can come from official reports, pilot observation, exploratory interviews, organizational documents, secondary data, or prior research (p.94).
- Recommended structure: an **evidence chain**, where every major claim in the background section has a traceable source (e.g., "process is slow" ← transaction logs; "delay is caused by serial approval" ← process mining + interviews; "delay lowers satisfaction" ← survey) (p.94–95, Fig 5.3).

## 5. Literature Review

**Location: BAB 6, whole chapter (p.103–116).**

- Literature review has a **dual function** in design research: **problem-side review** (explains the problem, causes, theory, stakeholders) and **solution-side review** (maps existing artifacts, methods, models, algorithms, frameworks, design principles, and their evaluation evidence) (p.103).
- Six functions (Table 6.1, p.104–105): problem understanding, knowledge mapping, solution mapping, justificatory knowledge (basis for design decisions — Gregor & Jones 2007), evaluation reference (metrics/criteria/baselines used previously), gap identification, contribution positioning.
- Should be **concept-centric, not author-centric** (Webster & Watson 2002, p.103).
- §6.2 **Search strategy** (p.104–106): build a conceptual search structure (concept blocks: problem / population-context / artifact-technology / outcome, each with synonyms/abbreviations), determine database coverage (Scopus, WoS, IEEE Xplore, ACM DL, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, etc., justified by domain), set defensible time bounds (separate *seminal knowledge* from *current state of the art* rather than blanket 5-year cutoffs), iteratively refine the search string, use **backward/forward snowballing**, and **document the entire search process** (databases, dates, strings, filters) for auditability.
- §6.3 **Systematic Literature Review (SLR)** (p.106–108, Kitchenham & Charters 2007): SLR ≠ narrative review mainly on **explicitness and reproducibility**. Three phases: planning (RQ + protocol) → conducting (search, selection, quality assessment, extraction, synthesis) → reporting. **PRISMA 2020 is explicitly stated to be a *reporting guideline*, not a substitute for the full SLR methodology** — the guideline flags this as a common misuse in proposals ("metode penelitian menggunakan PRISMA") (p.107, Page et al. 2021). SLR is **not mandatory** for every proposal — narrow domains with limited literature may justify a targeted literature review instead, as long as rigor is preserved (p.107).
- §6.4 **Protocol and selection process** (p.108–110): protocol components (Table 6.3, p.109–110): objective, review questions, databases, search strings, time span, inclusion/exclusion criteria, screening process, quality assessment, extraction form, synthesis plan, reporting plan. Selection stages: deduplication → title screening → abstract screening → full-text assessment, with documented counts at each stage.
- §6.5 **Extraction and synthesis** (p.110–111): extraction should separate **problem-side attributes** (context, stakeholders, causes, requirements, outcomes) from **solution-side attributes** (artifact type, architecture, mechanisms, technologies, evaluation, strengths/weaknesses). *Synthesis ≠ summary* — synthesis finds patterns, categories, relationships, and **contradictions** across studies, not just per-article recaps. An **artifact comparison matrix** (existing artifacts × requirements/capabilities) is recommended as a key output feeding directly into design gap identification (p.111).
- §6.6 **Bibliometrics** (p.112–113, Zupic & Čater 2015; Aria & Cuccurullo 2017): a **complement**, not a replacement, for substantive synthesis — citation counts show influence/visibility, not content quality or relevance. Has known limitations (citation lag, database coverage differences, author/keyword disambiguation) that must be reported if used.
- §6.7 **State of the art** (p.113–115): a synthesis of *what can currently be done, through which approaches, under what conditions, with what results, and what limitations* — **not** a list of recent articles. Built via defined comparison dimensions → taxonomy/clusters of approaches → capability/limitation assessment (limitations must have evidence, not just "not mentioned in the paper") → context/boundary conditions → final characterization (well-established / emerging / inconsistent / underexplored / absent).

## 7. Research Gap

**Location: BAB 7, whole chapter (p.117–130).**

- Central warning, stated explicitly and repeatedly: statements like *"belum ada penelitian di Indonesia," "masih sedikit penelitian," "belum digunakan metode X,"* or reusing another paper's "future work" line are **not automatically** a valid research gap — absence of a publication has many possible explanations (topic genuinely unstudied / not important / wrong search terms / database coverage / published under different terminology) (p.117–118, 122–123).
- §7.1 **Research gap concept** (p.117–119, Robinson et al. 2011): a gap should be characterized by the **nature of insufficiency** (insufficient/imprecise evidence, biased evidence, inconsistency, evidence not fit for the specific need) rather than mere absence. A valid research gap needs three elements: **(1) what is insufficient, (2) evidence that it is insufficient, (3) why the insufficiency matters** (p.119). Alvesson & Sandberg's (2011) *problematization* approach — challenging field-wide assumptions rather than just "spotting" an empty niche — is offered as an alternative, often stronger, gap-generation strategy (p.118).
- §7.2 **Knowledge gap** (p.119–120): more specifically about *content* (what is not known/understood), can be descriptive, explanatory, predictive, or prescriptive; often located in **mechanism–context–outcome** relationships (an artifact/mechanism works in context C, but is it known to work in context D?). Must be distinguished from a **personal** knowledge gap (the researcher doesn't know something the field already does) — literature review resolves this distinction (p.119–120).
- §7.3 **Practice gap vs. design gap** (p.120–122): *practice/practical gap* = current vs. desired state, in the world of practice. *Design gap* = existing artifacts lack a capability needed to close the practical gap — and **must be derived from requirements**, not free-standing ("if stakeholders don't need a capability, its absence is not a meaningful design gap," p.121). Table 7.2 (p.122) cleanly separates the three gap types by location, guiding question, and evidence basis. Gregor & Hevner's (2013) improvement / exaptation / invention framing is tied to the maturity of problem-domain vs. solution-domain knowledge (p.121–122).
- §7.4 **Identifying limitations of prior research** (p.122–124): four sources — (1) limitations stated by original authors (including "future work," but these must be re-checked against subsequent literature, not automatically adopted, p.122–123); (2) cross-study comparison (a limitation invisible in one paper but visible across several, e.g. everyone evaluates on the same dataset); (3) inconsistency between studies; (4) shared/unquestioned assumptions across a field (candidate for Alvesson & Sandberg problematization). Table 7.3 (p.123–124) categorizes limitations: data, method, context, theory, artifact, evaluation, measurement, assumption.
- §7.5 **Mapping existing solutions/artifacts** (p.124–126): unit-of-comparison must match artifact type (don't compare unlike artifact classes); comparison dimensions must derive from the problem/requirements (not "whatever is easy to find"); build an **artifact matrix** (rows = existing artifacts, columns = capabilities) — but "blank cell" must be read carefully: **"not reported" ≠ "not supported"** (p.124); compare **mechanisms, not just feature labels**; compare **strength of evaluation evidence** (expert opinion vs. field experiment are not equal-weight claims); absence of a capability *combination* is not automatically novelty — feasibility/value must still be checked (p.125–126).
- §7.6 **Identifying design opportunity** (p.126–127): design opportunity = intersection of practical relevance + knowledge grounding + feasibility + evaluability + novelty. Template sentence (p.127): *"Given practical problem P, knowledge/design limitation G, and available mechanism K, there is an opportunity to design artifact A with capability C, and evaluate whether it improves outcome O."*
- §7.7 **Formulating the gap** (p.127–129): five steps — state what is known → state what remains insufficient → explain why the gap matters → indicate a researchable response → **avoid overclaiming novelty**. Recommended prudent phrasing: *"within the reviewed literature," "the review identified limited evidence," "existing approaches reviewed do not simultaneously address…"* rather than absolute claims of "never done before" (p.128, explicitly flagged as a common overreach to avoid).

## 8. Research Questions

**Location: BAB 8 §8.1–8.3 (p.131–136).**

- Design research distinguishes **knowledge questions** (need to obtain knowledge) from **design problems/questions** (need to change the world to better fit stakeholder goals) (Wieringa 2014, p.132). A single design-research project can chain multiple RQ types: descriptive/explanatory (understand problem) → design-oriented (how should X be designed) → evaluative (how effective was it) (p.132–133).
- An RQ should **not embed an unproven solution**: *"How can AI be used to solve X?"* already assumes AI is the answer; better: *"How can a prioritization mechanism be designed to improve X?"* — technology is only locked in after state-of-the-art/design reasoning justifies it (p.132–133).
- Table 8.1 (p.133) types RQs: descriptive, explanatory, comparative, design-oriented, evaluative, mechanism-oriented, boundary-oriented, each with expected output type.
- §8.2 **RQ formulation** (p.133–135): RQ must be clear, specific, answerable, relevant, and scope-consistent; must have a defined unit of analysis/context, but should **not be so laden with implementation detail** that it becomes unreadable or over-locks the design. Number of RQs should be controlled — many proposals with 10–15 "RQs" are actually conflating research questions with methodological sub-activities. An **answerability test** is required: for each RQ, what data/evidence is needed, what method produces it, what form will the answer take (p.134).
- §8.3 **Problem–RQ linkage** (p.135–136): every RQ must have a **problem rationale** traceable to root cause / knowledge gap / design gap; every important claim about the artifact needs a corresponding evaluation question (an efficiency claim needs efficiency evidence, a fairness claim needs fairness evidence). Recommended tool: a **problem–RQ traceability matrix** (rows = practical problem/root causes/knowledge gap/design gap, columns = RQs) — used to find RQs with no problem origin, and problem elements with no RQ coverage (p.135–136).

## 9. Objectives

**Location: BAB 8 §8.4–8.5 (p.136–139).**

- Research objective = what the *research activity* will achieve (e.g., "evaluate the effectiveness of X"); **design objective** = the condition/capability the *artifact* must realize (e.g., "reduce waiting time while preserving fairness") — these are explicitly different levels and must not be conflated (p.137).
- Objectives must be at a level that can be checked — vague verbs like "understand comprehensively" or "develop an optimal system" are flagged as too weak; excessive metric detail belongs in requirements/evaluation criteria, not the objective statement itself (p.137).
- Per Peffers et al. (2007), objectives are derived from the problem definition **and** knowledge about what is feasible — not an unconstrained wish list (p.137).
- §8.5 (p.138–139) distinguishes four terms often used interchangeably: **Objective** (what the research aims to achieve), **Output** (directly produced result: model, dataset, prototype, method, paper), **Outcome** (the change that occurs when the output is used), **Contribution** (value added to practice/knowledge base). Table 8.3 (p.139) gives the full comparison with examples. A paper/publication is explicitly called a *communication medium*, not itself a problem solution or outcome (p.138). Full chain: **RQ → Objective → Research Activity → Output → Evidence → Contribution** (p.139).

## 10. Knowledge / Theory

**Location: BAB 9, whole chapter (p.144–155).**

- Theory's role in design research is **not limited to being tested** — five explicit functions (§9.1, p.144–146): (1) problem explanation, (2) requirement derivation, (3) design justification (citing Walls et al. 1992's *information systems design theory* as prescriptive+descriptive integration), (4) evaluation interpretation (explaining *why* an outcome occurred, not just that a metric moved), (5) supporting generalization (via mechanism/boundary-condition reasoning, not naive instance-to-population generalization).
- Two extremes to avoid: **theory-free design** (artifact with no explanatory grounding) vs. forcing one theory onto the whole project when it only fits part of the problem (p.145).
- §9.2 **Concept, construct, proposition, theory** (p.146–147): concept (abstract idea) → construct (more specifically defined concept, may itself be a form of artifact per March & Smith 1995) → proposition (a stated relationship between constructs, can be descriptive/explanatory/prescriptive) → theory (organizes constructs+propositions into explanation/prediction/prescription; Gregor 2006's typology of theory purposes is cited). Constructs used ambiguously (e.g., "fairness" without an explicit definition) are flagged as a source of ambiguous requirements/evaluation (p.146).
- §9.3 **Kernel theory / justificatory knowledge** (p.147–149): *kernel theory* (Walls et al. 1992) = theory providing the explanatory basis for a design prescription. *Justificatory knowledge* (Gregor & Jones 2007) is the **broader** term — need not be one formal theory; can be theory, empirical findings, or established principles, as long as it is strong enough to justify a design decision. Each justificatory knowledge source should be explicitly linked to the specific design decision it supports (a table format: knowledge source → insight → design implication → artifact component is recommended, p.147–148) — merely listing theories in the literature review without this linkage is called out as insufficient grounding (p.148).
- §9.4 **Theory selection criteria** (p.149–150): (1) problem relevance, (2) level of analysis match, (3) empirical support/maturity, (4) design relevance (does it yield actionable implications, not just explanation), (5) **parsimony** (use the minimum knowledge needed; more theories ≠ better).
- §9.5 **Integrating multiple theoretical perspectives** (p.150–151): justified when each theory has a distinct function (one explains problem mechanism, another informs design decision, a third explains user response); can integrate via complementarity or multi-level reasoning; conflicts between perspectives (e.g., efficiency theory vs. fairness principle) are explicitly valuable — they become design trade-offs and evaluation criteria, not something to hide.
- §9.6 **Building the conceptual framework** (p.151–152): steps — determine core constructs (only those with a direct function in explanation/design reasoning, not everything from the lit review) → define relationships (with explicit type: causal/association/dependency/moderation/sequence/design-influence) → add context/boundary conditions → connect descriptive and prescriptive sides (showing the intervention point) → source every relation (theory/empirical finding/preliminary study/design assumption — assumptions must be explicitly labeled as testable) → verify the framework actually drives requirements and evaluation (a "decorative" framework that doesn't influence either is a red flag, p.152).
- §9.7 **Framework → artifact link** (p.152–154): each artifact component should have a **design rationale** in the form: *"because knowledge K shows mechanism M affects outcome O, the artifact provides function F to influence M"* (p.153). The conceptual framework is a **working theory** expected to be revised after evaluation — full cycle: **knowledge → conceptual framework → design requirements → artifact mechanism → evaluation → refined knowledge** (p.153–154).

## 12. Design Requirements

**Location: BAB 10, whole chapter (p.156–167).**

- §10.1 (p.156–158): requirement origins are broader than direct user requests — can come from practical problem, root cause, stakeholder goal, regulation, theory, technical constraints, ethical values, or existing-artifact findings. Requirement ≠ solution feature: *"the system must use machine learning"* is normally a **design decision**, not a requirement, unless ML use is genuinely mandated by context; the solution-neutral form is preferred (e.g., "the system must be able to update priority when demand patterns change") so the technology choice stays open for design search (p.157). Requirement ≠ objective: objective states the desired condition; requirement states what the artifact must do/have to support it (p.157). References ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 as the requirements-engineering standard (p.157), and Pohl (2010) on the need for clear, verifiable requirements (p.157).
- §10.2 **Stakeholder needs** (p.158–159): 5-step process — identify relevant stakeholders (direct users, decision makers, operators, maintainers, regulators, data subjects) → distinguish *stated need* from *underlying goal* (e.g., "we need a dashboard" may really be about visibility into a bottleneck) → triangulate across multiple evidence sources (interviews, observation, process data, documents, regulation, workshops, surveys) → surface conflicts explicitly rather than hiding them → prioritize (e.g., MoSCoW, but the basis for prioritization must be explained).
- §10.3 **Functional vs. non-functional requirements** (p.159–160): functional = what the artifact must do; non-functional (NFR) = quality attributes/constraints (reliability, performance, security, usability, privacy, explainability, maintainability, interoperability, availability). The classification is a practical organizing tool, not always mutually exclusive (privacy can generate both a function and a quality constraint). NFRs matter especially because many **design trade-offs** live between quality attributes (accuracy↔explainability, security↔friction, aggressive prioritization↔fairness) — the proposal must state which trade-offs fall inside the evaluation plan (p.159). Requirements should have an **acceptance criterion** where feasible, though not every quality needs a numeric threshold at proposal stage (some, like interpretability, may require expert/user evaluation instead) (p.159–160). Requirement hierarchy: stakeholder goal → functional capability → quality requirement → technical constraint (p.160).
- §10.4 **Design requirements** (p.160–161): should be *solution-oriented enough to guide design* but not implementation-locking (e.g., "must consider urgency and complexity dynamically" is good; "must use XGBoost version X" over-locks unless already justified). Requirement sources should carry traceability codes (e.g., STK-01 for stakeholder, KB-02 for knowledge base, REG-01 for regulation, RC-01 for root cause) (p.160–161). Requirements must be tested for **necessity** (is it actually needed for the objective) and **sufficiency** (do all requirements together suffice for the objective) — sufficiency reasoning is expected even if it can't be formally proven (p.161). Requirements are compared against existing artifacts: if existing artifacts already satisfy all requirements, the need for a new artifact must be questioned — the design gap is exactly where an important capability is not adequately met (p.161).
- §10.5 **Design objectives** (p.161–162): derived from desired state + research objective, but focused on solution qualities; can be multi-dimensional and create trade-offs requiring an objective hierarchy or explicit constraints; "using AI" is explicitly rejected as a design objective (it's an approach, not a stakeholder-value-linked objective) (p.161–162). Objectives feed requirements in a many-to-many relationship, manageable via a traceability matrix (p.162).
- §10.6 **Design principles** (p.162–164, Gregor et al. 2020): prescriptive knowledge connecting **goal + context + mechanism** (+ human actors, complexity, causal assumptions, justificatory knowledge). Different from a requirement (which states *what* the artifact must satisfy) — a design principle gives guidance on *how a class of artifacts* should be designed, and is therefore a candidate **knowledge contribution** because of its higher abstraction/reusability (p.162–163). Trivial prescriptions ("make the system easy to use") are explicitly rejected as not being real design principles — a principle needs action/property + goal + mechanism + context (p.163). At proposal stage, principles may only exist as *candidate/preliminary* principles to be tested (p.163).
- §10.7 **Traceability** (p.164–165): five linked chains — problem→need, need→objective→requirement, requirement→artifact component, requirement→evaluation criterion, evaluation→contribution. A **requirements traceability matrix** (problem | need | objective | requirement | component | evaluation) is the recommended tool (Table 10.3, p.165), and is expected to evolve during the study with changes logged (reason + impact recorded as research evidence, not silently edited) (p.164).

## 13. Artifact

**Location: BAB 11, whole chapter (p.168–180).**

- §11.1 (p.168–169): artifact = something deliberately designed to achieve a purpose; must have a **purpose** and a **context of use** — a purely descriptive model is not a design artifact unless intended to help produce change. Bentuk artifact is a **consequence** of problem→requirements analysis, never chosen first (p.169) — explicit warning against deciding upfront "the research will build an application" without justifying why an application is the right solution form (p.169, also earlier at p.16–17).
- March & Smith's (1995) four classic output types — **constructs, models, methods, instantiations** — are given (p.168–169) alongside an expanded list the guideline adds: (5) framework, (6) reference architecture, (7) algorithm, (8) protocol, (9) ontology, (10) maturity model, (11) decision mechanism, (12) design principles, (13) combinations (p.17, restated p.168–169). Table 11.1/1.3-style mapping of form→function→example appears at p.19 and again per-section in BAB 11.
- §11.2 **Construct as artifact** (p.169–170): a contribution when existing concepts insufficiently represent an important phenomenon; needs conceptual clarity, domain, dimensions, and relation to existing constructs — inventing new terminology for an old concept without need is *not* a contribution (p.169–170).
- §11.3 **Model as artifact** (p.170–172): can be descriptive, predictive, prescriptive, computational, conceptual, mathematical, or simulation-based; must have an **intended use**, explicit **assumptions**, and undergo **validation** (logical consistency, predictive accuracy, decision quality, expert agreement, or outcome improvement — no single method fits all). Table 11.1 (p.171–172) lists 8 model types and functions.
- §11.4 **Method as artifact** (p.172–173, March & Smith 1995): a procedure to help stakeholders perform an activity; needs input, process, decision rules, output, roles, and conditions of use spelled out — a vague phrase like "use a data-driven approach" is explicitly **not** a method (p.173). Every major step should be traceable to a requirement or justificatory knowledge.
- §11.5 **Framework and architecture as artifact** (p.173–174): a **framework artifact** (a solution deliberately designed for stakeholder use) is explicitly distinguished from the **conceptual framework** of BAB 9 (a knowledge representation guiding the research) — a single study can have both (p.173). Framework needs purpose, scope, components, relationships, process of use, expected outcome — a diagram with a few labeled boxes is *not* a framework without an explanation of how it is used to achieve a goal (p.173). Architecture emphasizes structure/components/interfaces/dependencies. The guideline explicitly cautions against loose naming: if the actual output is just a list of factors, "model" or "taxonomy" may be more accurate than "framework" (p.174).
- §11.6 **Instantiation/prototype as artifact** (p.174–176, March & Smith 1995): prototypes need not be production-ready — fidelity should match the evaluation question (low-fidelity sufficient for interaction concepts, insufficient for latency testing). Prototype evolution across iterations should be documented (requirement changes, findings, decisions). Value is judged by how well the prototype embodies the **design mechanisms** under test, not by feature count or visual polish (p.175). Lifecycle-after-research (deployment vs. proof-of-concept) should be stated to avoid overclaiming readiness.
- §11.7 **Initial artifact specification (at proposal stage)** (p.175–177): six parts — (1) artifact purpose (problem, users, targeted outcome, chosen form, linked back to objectives), (2) high-level functions (from requirements; input–process–output or component diagrams, without locking implementation detail), (3) core mechanisms (the part expected to generate the *main* effect — evaluation should focus here since it is usually the novelty source), (4) architecture/component structure (each component tied to a requirement), (5) assumptions/constraints/**open design decisions** (explicitly stating what is *not yet* decided is preferred over an arbitrary premature decision — p.176), (6) planned evaluation (every major claim paired with an evaluation criterion). Framed as a **design hypothesis**: *"If artifact A provides mechanisms M1, M2, M3 in context C, outcome O is expected to improve because of justificatory knowledge K"* (template given verbatim, p.178, exercise item 10). Table 11.2 (p.176) gives the full initial-specification template (name, type, target problem, target users, design objectives, core requirements, core mechanisms, major components, inputs, outputs, constraints, assumptions, evaluation criteria, open decisions).

## 14. Development

**Location: BAB 12 §12.1–12.4, §12.7 (p.181–189, 192–194).**

- §12.1 **Design & development strategy** (p.181–184): design = search in a solution space (Simon 1996). Steps: translate requirements into design **functions** → generate **multiple design alternatives** (not settling on the first idea) → **trade-off analysis** across explicit criteria → select the **core design mechanism** (the part expected to carry the novelty/main effect — development effort should concentrate here, not on peripheral interface work, p.182) → incremental implementation (low fidelity first, raise fidelity once the core mechanism is shown feasible) → connect every major design decision to an evaluation question. Table 12.1 (p.183) gives the 8-stage strategy table.
- §12.2 **Build–evaluate cycle** (p.184–186, March & Smith 1995; Hevner 2007): iterative; each cycle should have an explicit **learning objective**, not just "version 2, version 3." Evaluation outcomes can trigger four different depths of change: implementation refinement, parameter/rule change, architecture change, or (most fundamental) revision of the requirement/conceptual assumption itself — these must be distinguished because they carry different implications for design knowledge (p.185). A **stopping rule** for iteration must be defined (critical requirements met, result stability, no critical defects, performance threshold reached) (p.185). The cycle should be logged (Table 12.2 example, p.185–186: iteration | design focus | evaluation | finding | change | learning).
- §12.3 **Prototyping** (p.186–187): fidelity levels — conceptual, low-fidelity, functional, high-fidelity/production-like, pilot — each with a stated fit and limitation (Table 12.3, p.187). Prototype also functions as a **boundary object** for eliciting stakeholder feedback that is hard to get from abstract requirements alone (p.187). Each prototype needs an explicit status/scope statement (proof-of-concept vs. experimental vs. pilot vs. production) to prevent overclaiming readiness.
- §12.4 **Data, technology, and development resources** (p.187–189): data must be assessed for **fitness for purpose** (large ≠ adequate if it doesn't represent the needed mechanism); unavailable data is a research risk to name explicitly. Technology choices should follow requirements, not novelty-for-its-own-sake, and need not be locked early unless they affect feasibility/evaluation. Human resources (domain experts, developers, statisticians, evaluators) and **access/authorization** (organizational entry, personal-data use, system-log access) are named as commonly neglected resource categories. A **resource–risk matrix** (resource | need | availability | risk | mitigation) is recommended (Table 12.4, p.188–189).
- §12.7 **Documentation of the design process** (p.192–194): design rationale should follow *issue → alternatives → criteria → decision → justification → evidence*; version control alone is insufficient — the *conceptual reason* for a change must be recorded, not just a commit message; **failed designs must be documented too** (negative results reveal boundary conditions and are explicitly valuable, not something to erase to create a false "it worked from the start" narrative) (p.193). Table 12.7 (p.193–194) lists 8 minimum documentation artifacts: design journal, decision log, requirement log, version log, test log, evaluation log, issue log, reflection note.

## 15. Demonstration

**Location: BAB 12 §12.5–12.6 (p.189–192).**

- §12.5 (p.189–190, Peffers et al. 2007): demonstration answers **"can the artifact be used / does the mechanism run?"** — explicitly distinct from evaluation, which answers **"how well does it work / does it meet objectives?"** (p.190, this distinction is repeated as important). Environments range from conceptual walkthrough → simulation → laboratory → sandbox → pilot organization → operational environment, trading off realism vs. control vs. risk (Table 12.5, p.190). Environment/configuration (data, parameters, versions, actors) must be documented for repeatability and later comparison with evaluation.
- §12.6 **Scenarios and use cases** (p.191–192): each scenario needs actor, initial condition, input, action, expected behavior, observable outcome. Scenario types: normal, edge, failure, conflict — a full set is expected, not just favorable cases; **explicit warning against cherry-picking** scenarios that make the artifact look good (p.191). Scenarios should be traced to requirements via a requirement–scenario matrix (Table 12.6 example, p.191–192) to check coverage. Demonstration data feeds forward into refinement and formal evaluation.

## 16. Evaluation

**Location: BAB 13, whole chapter (p.196–208).**

- §13.1 **Purposes of evaluation** (p.196–198, Hevner et al. 2004): seven distinct purposes, each with its own question (Table 13.1, p.198): **verification** ("built to spec?"), **validation** ("does it solve the intended problem?" — explicitly different from verification, an artifact can pass verification and still fail stakeholder needs, p.196–197), utility, quality, efficacy, **comparison** (vs. baseline/existing artifact/current process — needed to show improvement is substantial, not just "it functions"), **learning** (why did it work or fail — negative results can still generate knowledge), and support for the final **contribution claim**.
- §13.2 **Formative vs. summative** (p.198–199, Venable et al. 2016/FEDS): formative = improve the artifact during development (diagnostic: "what needs fixing?"); summative = judge a artifact at a given maturity level against objectives (judgment: "how well does it meet the goal?"). The distinction is about **purpose of use**, not timing — an early experiment can be summative for a specific design hypothesis, a late-stage test can still be formative if its result triggers refinement (p.199).
- §13.3 **Ex ante vs. ex post** (p.199–201, Blessing & Chakrabarti 2009 Impact Model): ex ante = before full implementation (expert judgment, modeling, simulation — reduces risk of discovering a fundamental flaw only after full build); ex post = after implementation/use (experiment, field use, logs, observation, interview — higher evidence realism but more expensive/confounded). Combination recommended: ex ante checks plausibility/feasibility, ex post tests whether the expected mechanism actually produced the outcome; **the gap between expected and observed behavior is itself a source of design learning** (p.200).
- §13.4 **Artificial vs. naturalistic** (p.201–202, Venable et al. 2016/FEDS): artificial = controlled (lab, simulation, synthetic dataset, benchmark) — high control/repeatability, lower realism; naturalistic = real/near-real use (field study, org pilot, action research) — high realism, lower control, more confounding. Not mutually exclusive — a strong strategy progresses **simulation → laboratory → pilot → field use**, each stage answering a different evidence question (p.202).
- §13.5 **Criteria, indicators, metrics** (p.202–203): criterion (dimension of success) → indicator (observable manifestation) → metric (measurement method) — three distinct levels, all must derive from requirements/objectives. Metrics need a **baseline** (a number is meaningless without a comparison point — existing system, prior model, standard, or threshold). Distinguishes **technical metric** (e.g., model accuracy) from **outcome metric** (e.g., actual service-delay reduction) — an artifact can improve a technical metric without improving the practical problem, so both should be evaluated when the claim spans both levels (p.203). Trade-off-sensitive metrics (distribution, subgroup, variance, worst-case, not just averages) are recommended where fairness-type claims are made (p.203).
- §13.6 **Data collection and analysis methods** (p.203–205): quantitative (experiment, log analysis, benchmark, simulation, survey scale, statistical test) vs. qualitative (interview, observation, focus group, think-aloud, document analysis) vs. mixed methods — mixed-methods **integration must be planned**, not just "collect both kinds of data." Explicit warning against **method-driven research** — choosing a familiar method (SEM, ML, thematic analysis) because it's familiar rather than because the RQ demands it (p.204). Data management (storage, anonymization, versioning, access control, retention) must be planned, especially for personal data.
- §13.7 **Validity, reliability, rigor** (p.205–206, Shadish/Cook/Campbell 2002 cited in ch. bibliography): **construct validity** (does the measurement really represent the intended construct — e.g., fairness measured only by average waiting time may not represent fairness), **internal validity** (how strongly can outcome changes be attributed to the artifact, esp. for causal claims), **external validity/transferability** (generalization via mechanism/boundary-condition reasoning, *not* naive statistical generalization from one case study), **reliability** (consistency of measurement/procedure — coding procedures, audit trails, triangulation for qualitative work). **Triangulation** — inconsistent results across sources/methods are not necessarily a problem; they can reveal genuine complexity/boundary conditions (p.206). Governing principle: **claim–evidence fit** — claim strength must match evidence strength (e.g., lab evaluation cannot support a national-scale impact claim; expert review cannot prove operational effectiveness) (p.206).

## 17. Learning

Treated as an explicit stage across multiple chapters rather than one dedicated section:
- BAB 1 §1.5 (p.19–21): knowledge formation occurs when build/evaluate results are analyzed not just to fix one artifact but to extract transferable lessons — principles that hold under some conditions, assumptions that turn out false, combinations that outperform.
- BAB 12 §12.2/§12.7 (p.184–186, 192–194): the build–evaluate cycle's explicit "Learn" step and the requirement to document design rationale/failed designs specifically to preserve learning as research evidence.
- BAB 13 §13.1 (p.197–198): "learning" listed as one of evaluation's seven purposes — explaining *why* an artifact worked or failed, including from negative results.
- BAB 14 Ringkasan (p.221–222): learning is folded into the final chain as part of contribution formation: **Problem → Evidence → Gap → RQ → Knowledge → Requirements → Artifact → Build → Evaluate → Learn → Contribute** (final summary diagram, p.225, item 14 of the closing exercise).

## 18. Contribution

**Location: BAB 8 §8.6–8.8 (p.139–142); BAB 14 §14.4–14.5 (p.214–216).**

- §8.6 **Practical contribution** (p.139–140, Hevner et al. 2004): value to stakeholders/the problematic situation — must be a **change/outcome**, not a feature list ("has a dashboard" is not a contribution; "managers can identify bottlenecks faster, enabling correction before SLA breach" is, p.139). Must be stated **relative to a baseline**. Three levels given (p.140): (1) proof the artifact can perform a previously unavailable function, (2) measurable outcome improvement, (3) organizational/societal-level impact — claim level must match the evaluation actually performed. Negative consequences/trade-offs must be weighed, not ignored (an artifact that speeds a process while increasing unfairness cannot be called successful on speed alone, p.140).
- §8.7 **Knowledge/academic contribution** (p.140–141, Gregor & Hevner 2013; Gregor & Jones 2007; Gregor, Chandra Kruse & Seidel 2020): contribution levels range from **situated implementation** (concrete, low transferability) up through **nascent design theory** (constructs/methods/models/design principles) to **well-developed design theory**. Design principles per Gregor et al. (2020) need goal + context + mechanism (a bare "use method X" is not yet a design principle without this). **Negative knowledge counts** — findings that a mechanism fails under certain conditions, or that a trade-off cannot be eliminated, are explicitly valid knowledge contributions (p.141). Contribution should be stated as a **target** at proposal stage, not a guaranteed result — final claims are only set after evaluation and reflection (p.141).
- §14.4 **Artifact–evaluation–contribution consistency** (p.214–215): every contribution claim must trace to specific artifact capability + specific evaluation evidence (a matrix format is recommended, Table 14.4, p.214–215). **Overclaim** is explicitly named as arising when evidence is too weak for the claim (e.g., simulation success ≠ organization-wide effectiveness claim, p.214). If the artifact does not reach its practical target, the research can still yield knowledge about mechanism/boundary conditions — a proposal whose *entire* contribution depends on artifact success is flagged as conceptually risky (p.214).
- §14.5 **Novelty/originality** (p.215–216, Gregor & Hevner 2013): novelty ≠ using the newest technology. Improvement / exaptation / invention framing restated. Novelty can live at the level of construct, model, method, mechanism, combination, evaluation approach, or design principle — Table 14.5 (p.216) enumerates all 9 forms with examples. Recommended novelty-statement structure: **Existing State → Limitation → Proposed Difference → Expected Value → Evidence Needed** (p.215–216). Prudent phrasing again emphasized over absolute "no one has ever" claims (p.215).

## 19. Feasibility

**Location: BAB 14 §14.6 (p.216–217).**

- Research plan must show sequencing/dependency logic, not just a Gantt chart cosmetic exercise — e.g., problem investigation before final requirements, development before summative evaluation, but literature review/documentation/formative evaluation can overlap (p.216).
- Resource planning categories: data, equipment, software, computing, access, budget, stakeholders, expertise — critical resources need a **contingency plan** (p.216).
- Risk categories: methodological, technical, organizational, data-related, ethical, schedule-related — each risk scored by probability × impact with a stated mitigation. Table 14.6 (p.217) is a worked risk register example (data incompleteness, pilot delay, prototype delay, low recruitment, unstable metric, scope creep — each with probability/impact/mitigation/contingency).
- Schedules should include **time buffer for iteration** — design research is rarely linear, and an overly tight Gantt chart with no refinement time is called out as commonly unrealistic (p.217).

## 20. Ethics

**Location: BAB 14 §14.7 (p.217–218).**

- Design-research ethics covers not only participant protection but also the **consequences of the artifact itself** on decisions, access, privacy, fairness, safety, and organizational behavior — ethical consideration should surface as early as the requirements stage, not as a late administrative add-on (p.217).
- Human-subject research needs informed consent, voluntary participation, confidentiality, data protection, and risk disclosure per applicable regulation/policy.
- Data-based artifacts need data minimization, access control, retention policy, and appropriate security — "might be useful someday" is explicitly rejected as sufficient justification for collecting personal data (p.217).
- Algorithmic artifacts need attention to bias/fairness — evaluation should check for unjustified outcome differences across groups; human oversight may be required for high-impact decisions (p.218).
- Academic integrity: accurate citation, truthful reporting, no fabrication/falsification/plagiarism, **negative results must not be hidden** just because they don't support the design hypothesis (p.218).
- **Generative-AI use in research/writing** must follow institutional/publisher policy; AI cannot be an author; the researcher remains responsible for accuracy, originality, citation, and confidentiality of any material processed through it (p.218). This is the guideline's only explicit statement on AI-assisted research conduct.
- Design decisions adapted from an existing principle require attribution, same as any other borrowed idea (p.218).
- Overall framing: an artifact that is effective but violates privacy, fairness, or academic integrity **cannot be considered a good research contribution** (p.218).

## 21. Proposal Readiness

**Location: BAB 14 §14.2–14.3, §14.8 (p.211–214, 218–221).**

- §14.2 **Problem–gap–RQ–objective consistency** (p.211–212): every RQ must trace to a gap; every important gap must be answered by some RQ; every objective must be tested against its RQ (a free-standing objective is usually a misplaced activity/deliverable); **terminology must stay consistent** (a term introduced in the problem statement should not silently change in the RQ — "conceptual drift" flagged as a common source of confusion in requirements/measurement, p.211); scope must stay consistent (gap found at a national level but evaluation done at one local unit is acceptable *only if the contribution claim is scoped down accordingly* — narrowing methods while overgeneralizing conclusions is explicitly the failure mode to avoid, p.211–212). Table 14.2 (p.212) gives a problem–gap–RQ–objective–evidence/method traceability template.
- §14.3 **Requirements–artifact–development consistency** (p.212–213): a requirement without an artifact component means no design response exists; a component without a requirement raises the question of why it exists; development method must fit the artifact form (framework development ≠ algorithm development ≠ socio-technical system development, each needs different methods named explicitly — naming "DSRM" alone does not explain *how* the artifact was actually built, p.213). Table 14.3 (p.213) gives a requirement–mechanism–component–development-activity–verification matrix template.
- §14.8 **Final readiness checklist** (p.218–221): seven guiding questions before submission — (1) is the problem actually proven (evidence/stakeholder/current-desired state/consequence/significance all findable without guessing)? (2) is the gap valid (state-of-the-art strong enough, novelty not just self-declared)? (3) is every RQ answerable (method/data/expected evidence stated)? (4) does the artifact have a rationale (requirements/justificatory knowledge/mechanism/components/development strategy all consistent)? (5) is the evaluation sufficient for the claims (criteria/metrics/baseline/data/method/analysis/validity threats all stated)? (6) is it feasible (schedule/access/resources/ethical approval/technology/data all realistic)? (7) does **end-to-end traceability** hold with no broken links from problem to contribution? Table 14.7 (p.219–220) is the **full 17-item readiness checklist** (context, problem, stakeholders, gap, RQ, objectives, theory, requirements, artifact, development, demonstration, evaluation, contribution, novelty, ethics, feasibility, traceability) — each phrased as a yes/no verification question, used directly as the template for `PROPOSAL_GUIDELINE_AUDIT.md` in Phase 1.

## Not Addressed by the Guideline (explicit gaps — do not fabricate)

The following items appear nowhere in the 225-page source and must **not** be treated as covered by this guideline:

- **No numeric scoring rubric (1–5 scale) is defined by the guideline itself for readiness assessment** — Table 14.7 (p.219–220) is a yes/no checklist, not a 1–5 scale. (Note: the *later* phases of the user's master plan, e.g. Phase 12 "Readiness Check," specify their own 1–5 scoring scheme — that scheme is the *user's* addition, not something drawn from this PDF, and must be clearly labeled as such if used.)
- **No discussion of specific statistical test selection** (e.g., when to use McNemar vs. t-test vs. Fisher's exact) — the book stays at the level of "quantitative/qualitative/mixed methods" and "validity/reliability" principles, not specific test mechanics.
- **No treatment of thesis/dissertation defense procedure, examination format, or committee structure** at ITB or elsewhere.
- **No discussion of specific citation style (APA/IEEE/etc.) formatting rules** — citation is discussed only at the level of "accurate attribution," not a style guide.
- **No treatment of computer-vision-, machine-learning-, or FER-specific methodological concerns** (e.g., cross-validation design, embedding models, dataset bias in vision tasks) — the book is a domain-general design-research proposal guideline (with a service-operations toy example, SPLAA); it does not address any AI/ML-specific research design concerns.
- **No page count, formatting template, or submission-mechanics guidance** (margins, font, page limits) for the actual proposal document.
- **No explicit discussion of qualitative-only or purely explanatory (non-design) thesis formats** — the entire book assumes a design-research orientation; it does not describe how a non-design (purely explanatory/behavioral) research proposal should be structured, beyond the brief orientation contrast in §1.1.
- **No specific guidance on multi-supervisor or co-supervision dynamics**, funding-proposal specific requirements (e.g., grant budget justification formats), or industry-sponsored research contracts.
