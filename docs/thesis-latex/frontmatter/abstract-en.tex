Facial Expression Recognition (FER) models are usually developed on benchmark data that do not represent all user groups, so their performance can differ between groups. This research is motivated by the long-term goal of building an FER system that is inclusive of users with both light and dark skin. However, the data available in this research cover only the Dark and Medium-Dark groups from one Papuan documentary film, so this thesis restricts itself to evaluating the performance gap between these two groups and does not test lighter skin.

The primary data consist of 135 manually labeled face crops in five expression classes (Protocol A). Brightness groups are computed from facial pixel luminance and treated as a proxy, not as a validated measure of skin tone or ethnicity, and not as ground truth. Three research questions are answered by re-analyzing saved predictions, with 95\% confidence intervals (CI) from a grouped bootstrap over temporal blocks to account for correlation between crops from the same scene. The gap is defined as the accuracy difference between the Dark and Medium-Dark groups.

The first question assesses the pretrained HSEmotion model without retraining. It reaches an accuracy of 0.363 and a macro F1 of 0.249; its accuracy is 0.483 on Dark crops and 0.130 on Medium-Dark crops, a gap of 0.353 (95\% CI [0.182, 0.540]). The gap persists within the same class and comes largely from Neutral expressions predicted as Angry in the Medium-Dark group (12 of 21).

The second question explores one adaptive CLAHE preprocessing configuration. This configuration changes the gap by $-$0.023 (95\% CI [$-$0.136, 0.073]). Because the confidence interval includes zero, the effect of CLAHE on the gap cannot be determined from this sample; this result does not show that CLAHE improves fairness.

The third question retrospectively compares a Logistic Regression classifier on frozen ArcFace embeddings. This classifier reaches an accuracy of 0.407 and a macro F1 of 0.272, with no significant difference in overall accuracy from HSEmotion (McNemar's test, $p = 0.4614$). Its gap is smaller, 0.123 (95\% CI [$-$0.038, 0.282]), because of higher Medium-Dark accuracy. However, the difference between the two models' gaps excludes zero under only one of the two groupings examined, so this reduction is suggestive and not established.

As a separate sensitivity analysis (Protocol B), 227 crops were relabeled with facial landmarks used as a visual aid. Of these, 148 crops were labeled Ambiguous, leaving only 72 crops in four classes usable. The retrained ArcFace classifier reaches an accuracy of 0.611 (95\% CI [0.528, 0.714]), with a gap that cannot be distinguished from zero. Protocol B uses different labels, crops and class sets, so its results are not directly comparable with Protocol A and show neither a model improvement nor a fairness improvement. What Protocol B shows is the sensitivity of evaluation results to labels: the same HSEmotion predictions reach an accuracy of 0.279 against the first-round labels but 0.162 against the second-round labels on the same 68 crops.

Compared with earlier FER fairness studies, which mostly use large benchmark data with annotated demographic attributes, this thesis evaluates real data from an under-represented population using a brightness proxy and confidence intervals that account for the temporal structure of the data. All results are associations in one small sample from one film with one annotator. This thesis does not evaluate lighter skin, does not infer causation, and does not generalize its results to other populations. A facial-landmark annotation track (Papuan V2) is documented as supporting work and is not used as performance evidence.

The contributions of this thesis are a gap evaluation that is reproducible from saved artifacts, documentation of a Neutral-to-Angry error pattern concentrated in the Medium-Dark group, careful reporting of two simple interventions that distinguishes inconclusive, suggestive and established results, and a sensitivity analysis showing how strongly FER evaluation results depend on labels and image-quality filtering.
